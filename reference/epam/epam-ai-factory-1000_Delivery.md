# Role Context

**1 — Role names and who they apply to**

The role this module describes belongs to whoever owns the commitment and the cadence of one or more teams — the schedule, the budget, the staffing, and the way adoption of working practices happens day to day. Common job titles:

- **Delivery Manager (DM)** — accountable for delivery commitment, budget, margin, and adoption at the engagement level; usually owns one large team or several smaller ones.

- **Project Manager (PM)** — owns one project's plan, status, risk and change at the working level; usually closer to the day-to-day than a DM.

- **Scrum Master (PM-flavoured)** — when the cadence is run by an Agile practitioner; the duties below still apply, only with different ceremonies.

If your title varies, the test is functional: if you own delivery commitment, staffing, budget, and adoption of working practices for one or more teams, this role is yours. But the Wide path is written for **everyone on the team**, not only role-holders — every other role reads it to understand what the role does and what their own work feeds it, even without ever carrying the title. DM and PM are taught together because in an AI-native team they share the same craft surface — the proposal pack, the rollout plan, the weekly status memo — and the work splits by depth of accountability, not by job title.

**2 — Role description and core duties**

The DM/PM owns the surface between *"we should do this engagement"* and *"the engagement delivers, the team learns, and we have evidence of both."* In observable verbs:

- **Qualifies** opportunities — fit, win themes, deal-breakers, top risks — and recommends bid / no-bid / bid-with-conditions.

- **Shapes** the proposal — solution outline (with deliberate outsourcing where capability is missing), staffing variants, estimate with separate contingency and margin, rollout plan, AI-native delivery section.

- **Commits** delivery dates and budgets; runs the governance and reporting cycle; manages scope, risk, and change against the baseline.

- **Owns adoption** of AI-native delivery practices on the team: conducts evidence-based maturity assessment, defines targets per SDLC phase, protects the Champion network, tracks AI costs.

- **Runs lessons-learned** that produce version-controlled artefacts (the L3 retro standard — not "we discussed it").

- **Escalates** when delivery, quality, scope, cost, or governance moves off track — to the client, to the executive sponsor, to legal/compliance as required.

What this role does *not* own: the spec (PROD/BA owns it), the architecture (Architecture owns it), the code (Engineering owns it), the test pyramid (QA owns it). The DM/PM owns the *commitment* on top of that work — the dates, the dependencies, the way handoffs happen, and the adoption the team is graded on.

**7 — What changed at L3 (AI-native)**

The team operates as an AI Factory: reusable context, prompts, playbooks, rules, and agent instructions live under **version control as code**, are reviewed on a cadence, and are deprecated on a policy. **Every core role has an AI Champion.** Daily active AI usage is above 80% and the metric is read alongside artefact-creation and cross-team-consumption rates so it can't be gamed. The manager's job restructures from *doing the work* to **specifying the work, reviewing the agent's output, and governing the system.** The bid is shaped to that future state — the proposal pack names target maturity per SDLC phase and how adoption will be measured, not "we'll use AI."

## Block 1 — What the role actually does

The DM/PM owns the surface between *"the team should do this work"* and *"the work delivered, the team learned, and there is evidence of both."* The daily job is a concrete set of activities that recur every sprint, not a quarterly event. Three run continuously:

- **Scope, risk & issue management** — a live risk register, scored by probability × impact and updated on a cadence, not populated at kick-off and forgotten. A risk that surfaces in week 8 and reaches a stakeholder in week 11 is a management failure, not bad luck.

- **Customer & stakeholder relations** — a communication plan with named cadences, run every week, not one status email to everyone. Silence is not evidence the relationship is healthy — only evidence the manager has stopped reading it.

- **Delivery execution** — sprint management, blocker removal, and meeting summaries with tracked actions. A blocker that sits three days with no removal attempt is an unmanaged risk.

The three are not separate: a scope change arrives as a stakeholder problem that blocks a sprint, and the manager works all three at once.

## Block 2 — The three levers

A DM/PM operates three levers, and the L3 distinction is that all three are *measured*, not merely claimed.

<img src="media/image3.png" style="width:6.60417in;height:2.08861in" />

*Source: EPAM AI Run program team*

- **Direction** — the team knows where it is going and whether it is on track, with evidence rather than the manager's assurance. The roadmap names artefacts and owners, not only phases and dates; the status report carries a go-to-green action per red item, not a RAG colour alone; the maturity roadmap names which SDLC phase sits at which level, cites the artefact behind the score, and assigns a named owner to each gap. The characteristic failure is the commitment nobody can grade — a risk register with no treatment owner, a maturity claim with no artefact behind it.

- **Economics** — the manager knows what delivery costs, what it produces, and whether the trade-offs are defensible. [AI costs](https://eu.git.epam.com/airun-mission-26-ai-factory/curriculum/-/blob/main/modules/000-glossary.md#ai-costs) are read as a delivery metric in the dashboard, beside throughput and quality — what a maturity jump cost per team, per feature, per model — not buried in a finance sheet. The characteristic failure is [contingency disguised as margin](https://eu.git.epam.com/airun-mission-26-ai-factory/curriculum/-/blob/main/modules/000-glossary.md#contingency-reserve): conflate the two and the risk reserve becomes invisible while the profit line flatters.

- **Conditions** — the environment that makes the other two possible: context-engineering standards (rule files, prompt libraries, reusable templates), artefact standards (what is version-controlled, who reviews it, when it is deprecated), the named [AI Champion](https://eu.git.epam.com/airun-mission-26-ai-factory/curriculum/-/blob/main/modules/000-glossary.md#ai-champion) network, and an onboarding path that reaches productive AI use in days, not weeks. The characteristic failure is mistaking access for adoption — a team where everyone holds a licence but prompts live in private chat histories is at L1, not L2, whatever the invoice says.

All three depend on inputs the manager does not produce alone. Direction needs planning assumptions, honest maturity self-assessments, and capacity numbers from the roles doing the work. Economics needs role-level data — approximate timesheets turn the cost-per-feature figure into fiction. Conditions improve every time a role version-controls its prompts, acceptance criteria, and carry-forward artefacts. A manager who invents these inputs is flying blind; the [AI-SDLC Maturity Framework](https://leap.epam.com/sp/ai-maturity-program/framework) only scores honestly when the artefacts behind it exist.

## Block 3 — Reading the stakeholder field

Stakeholder work is a core daily activity, not a soft skill the manager happens to have. Three habits keep it honest at L3.

First, **map stakeholders by *interest × influence*, not by org chart**. A high-influence, low-interest VP needs a different cadence than a high-interest, low-influence product owner — the same title can sit in two different quadrants. The grid implies four postures: manage the high-interest, high-influence quadrant closely; keep the high-influence, low-interest quadrant satisfied; keep the high-interest, low-influence quadrant informed; and monitor the rest.

Second, **name a *key concern* per stakeholder** — the specific thing that would make this person block or escalate ("a regulator audit in Q3 with no evidence of test coverage", not "quality").

Third, **name an *engagement signal* per stakeholder** — the observable behaviour that says the relationship is healthy or drifting: response time on reviews, attendance at the steering call, a tone shift in written feedback. Two missed signals in a row is the early warning; waiting for the escalation is too late.

The communication plan is the output of that map — one weekly email to everyone means the map was skipped. Stakeholder failure usually starts upstream: when a stakeholder hears about a scope change from someone other than the manager, the trust cracks. Information that belongs in the stakeholder loop has to reach the manager early enough to manage it.

## Block 4 — Where AI helps, and where the human owns the call

AI now assists every recurring delivery deliverable that has a template. What it does not do is make the call on the right-hand column below.

| **AI assists**                                                 | **Human-owned (non-negotiable)**                                           |
|----------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Drafting status memos, meeting summaries, retro analysis       | Strategic decisions on team direction, client positioning, programme scope |
| Risk identification and dependency surfacing from project data | Resource and capacity decisions (who works on what, at what cost)          |
| Estimation drafts and historical-comparable lookups            | Escalation calls on cost, quality, governance, or client risk              |
| Backlog clustering, defect dedup, action tracking              | Performance-management conversations                                       |
| Onboarding-doc drafting, FAQ surfacing                         | Contract changes and client commitments                                    |
| Dashboard commentary, weekly readout assembly                  | Risk acceptance at portfolio level                                         |
| RFP drafting, proposal-section writing                         | Hiring, team composition, AI Champion designation                          |
| Anomaly detection in delivery and product metrics              | What a metric anomaly means for the client relationship                    |

The right-hand column is not where AI is blocked — it is where the manager owns the outcome and signs the call. AI drafts options, surfaces precedent, and summarises context on every item there; the decision still travels through the human, because the failure mode of getting one wrong is real harm to a person, a client, or a regulatory obligation. *"The agent recommended it"* is not a defence that survives a review. And as agents take on more of the left-column sub-tasks, they run on the rest of the team's specifications, acceptance criteria, and context documents. Vague inputs produce unreliable output that needs human rework; clear, version-controlled context produces output the next agent can build on instead of redo.

## Block 5 — How the team is graded

Two models sit behind every maturity claim. The first is *what* gets scored: the four dimensions of the [AI-SDLC Maturity Framework](https://leap.epam.com/sp/ai-maturity-program/framework), each rated L1–L3. The L1→L3 bands below — including the \>80% daily-active-usage threshold — are the framework's, not numbers invented here. A maturity score is a *profile* across the four, scored at team level — not one number — and it is only honest when the artefacts behind each dimension exist.

| **Dimension**                | **What it measures**            | **L1 (At Risk)**                                        | **L2 (Baseline)**                                                                    | **L3 (Frontier)**                                                                  |
|------------------------------|---------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **AI Capabilities**          | how far AI runs the work        | assisted (deliverables made with AI help, results vary) | augmented (\>50% of core-role deliverables made and verified with AI)                | agentic (agents handle sub-tasks; new ways of working)                             |
| **Reusability**              | whether assets compound         | siloed (local prompts and scripts used by individuals)  | shared (prompts, rules, context, custom agents reused; onboarding materials updated) | codified (rules and custom agents maintained as code; automated improvement loops) |
| **AI Champions**             | who drives adoption on the team | sporadic (enthusiasts acting without a mandate)         | designated (at least one champion per team)                                          | embedded (all core roles represented; a champions network)                         |
| **Performance Tracking**     | whether adoption is measured    | partial (anecdotal evidence, no standard metrics)       | tracked (productivity metrics defined and measured consistently)                     | governed (AI cost tracked; reviews drive optimisations to output per total cost)   |
| **Daily Active Usage (DAU)** | how routine AI use is           | low (occasional use)                                    | majority (\>70% DAU)                                                                 | prevalent (\>80% DAU)                                                              |

Most projects should aim for L2. An assessment is not required at L1, but is required at L2 and L3 — the level a team claims has to be scored against the rubric with the artefacts behind it.

## Block 6 — The two-way contract

L3 delivery runs both ways. The manager owes the team a maturity roadmap with named owners, adoption metrics with defined denominators, AI cost visible in the dashboard, clear escalation paths, and a named **executive sponsor** with written authority to unblock policy or budget. In return, the system needs evidence that lands in every role's own work, every sprint:

- **Carry-forwards in version control**, not personal drives — if the manager has to book time to recover context, the artefact does not exist yet.

- **Clear acceptance criteria** — testable and unambiguous; the agent that drafts the next sprint backlog relies on them.

- **Documented decisions** — a Decision Memory entry with alternatives and rationale, not "we agreed in a chat"; a scope choice cannot be defended to a sceptical stakeholder from a private conversation.

- **Honest maturity self-reports with evidence** — the framework's dimensions can only be scored if the artefacts exist; the work is the evidence.

- **Retros that produce artefacts** — a retro that ends with no written output does not compound; a bi-weekly cadence × ≥1 artefact is 26 reusable artefacts a year.

<img src="media/image6.png" style="width:6.60417in;height:2.123in" />

A manager cannot honestly claim L2 if no artefacts exist; a team that produces them makes the claim defensible. The contract is concrete per role:

| Your role      | What your work gives the delivery system                 | Lever it feeds         |
|----------------|----------------------------------------------------------|------------------------|
| PROD / BA      | testable acceptance criteria; documented scope decisions | Direction              |
| Design         | journey and usability requirements                       | Direction              |
| Architecture   | decision records with rationale and rejected options     | Direction / Conditions |
| Engineering    | version-controlled specs and context; PR evidence        | Conditions / Economics |
| QA             | test evidence, golden sets, coverage numbers             | Direction              |
| Data           | feature definitions; data-quality numbers                | Economics              |
| Infrastructure | gateway logs (the AI-costs source); runbooks             | Economics              |
| Security       | the scored risk register and evidence pack               | Direction              |

**Anti-Patterns**

- **The kick-off risk register.** Written in week one and never updated, with no cadence, owner, or probability score → risks surface as crises because nobody was watching. *Done right:* score probability × impact and review on a fixed cadence, with a named owner per risk.

- **RAG without a route back to green.** A red item carries a colour but no corrective action → the report records the problem and changes nothing. *Done right:* every red carries a named go-to-green action, an owner, and a date.

- **Maturity claims without artefact evidence.** "We're at L2" with nothing in version control → the claim collapses the moment a buyer asks for proof. *Done right:* cite the artefact behind each level.

- **One cadence for all stakeholders.** A single weekly email to everyone → the high-influence exec and the low-interest reviewer are both served badly. *Done right:* derive the cadence from the interest × influence map.

- **Contingency absorbed into margin.** Risk reserve and profit on one line → when a risk materialises both vanish together and neither was visible beforehand. *Done right:* keep contingency and margin as separate lines.

- **Decisions that live in chat.** Scope agreed verbally and never written down → the rationale is gone when the question resurfaces, and no record exists for anyone who was not in the room. *Done right:* capture a Decision Memory entry with alternatives and rationale.

**Outcomes**

By the end of Wide Theory you can:

1.  Name the three levers a DM/PM operates (Direction, Economics, Conditions) and the evidence each one requires.

2.  Describe the DM/PM's recurring activities and why they run every sprint rather than at project milestones.

3.  Identify the five things every role's work owes the delivery system (carry-forwards, clear acceptance criteria, documented decisions, honest maturity evidence, artefact-producing retros).

4.  Tell a measured L3 commitment from an asserted one, and name what a sceptical reviewer would attack first.

This pattern indicates **unbalanced local optimization** \[faros.ai\]. At the **L2 (AI-Augmented)** maturity level, individual software developers are empowered with AI coding assistants (such as GitHub Copilot or Cursor) to write code and generate commits much faster than before \[agility-at-scale.com, faros.ai\]. Essentially, your team has accelerated their "local code generation" velocity, but their downstream validation mechanisms (CI/CD pipelines, automated testing, and human peer reviews) have buckled under the load \[agility-at-scale.com, faros.ai\].

**2. Why It Is a Risk Signal (Rather than a Success Signal)**

While a 15% throughput boost looks highly successful on paper, the concurrent 8% spike in change-failure rate makes this a severe risk signal for several reasons:

- **The Illusion of Velocity (The Rework Trap):** Merging code faster is meaningless if it breaks production. The time developers save writing code is quickly eaten up by triaging incidents, debugging, and rushing out hotfixes, erasing any real productivity gains \[agility-at-scale.com, faros.ai\].

- **The "Amplifier Effect":** According to DORA research, AI is an amplifier—it magnifies whatever engineering foundation you give it \[agility-at-scale.com\]. If a team has weak automated testing or fragile architecture, AI will not fix those issues; it will simply push more buggy code through those gaps at high speed \[agility-at-scale.com, faros.ai\].

- **Review Fatigue and "Rubber-Stamping":** Human peer-review processes are designed for human-paced software development. When flooded with a massive wave of PRs, human reviewers experience fatigue. To clear the queue, they may reduce scrutiny, resulting in subtle architectural flaws or context-misses slipping into production \[agility-at-scale.com, faros.ai\].

- **Escalated Code Churn:** Industry telemetry indicates that L2 teams often see their code churn rate (code that is rewritten or deleted within 14 days of being merged) spike dramatically, pointing to a severe drop in initial code quality \[getdx.com, faros.ai\].

**3. Investigation Checklist: Before Concluding the Team Has Reached L2 AI Capabilities**

To determine whether this imbalance is genuinely caused by reaching **L2 AI Capabilities**—or if it is merely a symptom of traditional process dysfunctions (such as end-of-quarter crunch pressure)—you should investigate the following dimensions:

**A. Tool Telemetry and Adoption**

- **What to look for:** Inspect IDE telemetry (e.g., Copilot or Cursor dashboard reports) to verify the actual adoption rate. Are developers accepting a high volume of AI code suggestions?

- **Why:** You must confirm that the 15% throughput surge is mathematically correlated with the usage of AI tools, rather than other factors like a sudden team expansion or an increase in minor, low-effort tasks.

**B. Pull Request (PR) Metrics and Review Friction**

- **What to look for:** Look at the average number of comments per PR, the PR rejection rate, and the cycle time from PR creation to merge.

- **Why:** If PR creation times have plummeted but review times have ballooned—or if the number of comments per PR has dropped while the change-failure rate spiked—it confirms that human peer-review fatigue is the bottleneck allowing flawed AI-generated code into the codebase \[faros.ai\].

**C. Code Churn vs. Static Quality**

- **What to look for:** Analyze git history to see if the 8% CFR is driven by high **code churn** (code updated/reverted within 14 days) \[getdx.com, faros.ai\].

- **Why:** AI assistants are highly capable of generating syntactically correct code that is contextually or architecturally flawed \[agility-at-scale.com, faros.ai\]. High code churn is a strong indicator of AI-generated code that compiled correctly but broke system logic once integrated \[getdx.com, faros.ai\].

**D. The Health of Downstream Automated Quality Gates**

- **What to look for:** Measure automated test coverage, test suite execution time, and the volume of "flaky tests."

- **Why:** At L2, automated testing pipelines are typically outdated and incapable of absorbing the high-velocity output \[agility-at-scale.com\]. If your test suites are slow or skipped, developers will bypass them, driving up CFR \[agility-at-scale.com, faros.ai\].

**E. Rule Out Confounding Non-AI Factors**

- **What to look for:** Did the team recently change their sprint scope definitions, onboard several new developers, experience a major reorganization, or push through "crunch-mode" pressure to hit quarterly goals?

- **Why:** You must rule out traditional management dysfunctions. A sprint throughput spike accompanied by a CFR spike can easily be caused by management pushing the team to sacrifice quality for speed, independent of AI tools.

**Transitioning Beyond the L2 Trap**

If your investigation confirms the team has hit L2, the next step is helping them transition toward **Level 3 (L3 / AI-Integrated) capabilities** \[agility-at-scale.com\]. This requires shifting focus from "writing code faster" to upgrading quality infrastructure: implementing robust CI/CD pipelines, relying on automated test suites, shrinking PR batch sizes, and making internal documentation AI-accessible \[agility-at-scale.com\].
